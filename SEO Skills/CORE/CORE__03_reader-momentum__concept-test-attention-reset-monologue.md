# Concept Test, Attention Reset, and Reader's Internal Monologue

**Scope: Universal.** Three rules that govern the overall page architecture: the concept test (does the proposition pass the smell test?), the attention reset (visual break every 3 sections), and the reader's 9-question monologue (every page must map to it).

---

## The Concept Test

Before writing a single line of HTML, every page must pass a one-sentence concept test:

> "Can the core proposition of this page be stated in one sentence that a reader would repeat to another reader?"

If the answer is no, the page concept needs sharpening before any copy is written.

### Why the test exists

A page that can't be summarised in one sentence is a page that doesn't have a clear proposition. The page may have lots of content, but without a single sticky idea, the reader leaves without anything to remember. They don't tell anyone about it; the page contributes nothing to organic word-of-mouth, social sharing, or branded search.

A page that can be summarised in one sentence — one that the reader would actually repeat — is a page with leverage. It punches above its weight in branded search, in social, in citations.

### Passing examples

- "Your Leeds property could earn 152% more per month on short-term letting than on a long-let — and Stayful manages everything."
- "Most Nottingham landlords switching from long-let are earning more within their first 90 days."
- "The honest income comparison for short-letting your Sheffield property — including the slow months."

### Failing examples

- "Stayful provides comprehensive Airbnb management services across [City]." (describes the service, not the proposition — no one would repeat it)
- "We're the leading short-let management company in the area." (positioning, not proposition — no specific claim)
- "Maximise your rental income with our expert team." (generic, applies to every competitor)

### When to run the test

- Before any Phase 2 work on a new page
- During Phase 1 audit if the page is failing to rank for its primary keyword
- Quarterly review of priority pages — has the proposition drifted?

A page that no longer passes the test needs a re-concept, not a content refresh.

## The Attention Reset Rule

A reader who has been scrolling for more than three consecutive sections without encountering a visually distinct, high-information element will **begin to skim or leave**.

The page structure must include an attention reset at least every three prose sections.

### What counts as an attention reset

- Stat row
- Pull stat
- Income comparison panel
- Seasonality bar chart
- SVG visual (demand catchment map, comparison card)
- Competitor comparison table
- Uplift component
- Owner testimonial pull quote

These components serve two purposes simultaneously:

1. They deliver high-density information in a format that requires less cognitive effort than prose
2. They visually break the page in a way that signals "something new and specific is here"

### What does NOT count

- An accordion (still text, just hidden)
- A CTA button (a transition, not a content reset)
- A simple bullet list (still text-density)
- A blockquote (still text)

The attention reset is specifically a **visual format change** that delivers content, not just structural separation.

### The rule in practice

Before marking any page ready for publication: count the prose sections between each visual component. If any gap exceeds three, a component is either missing or misplaced.

### The misuse rule

The attention reset rule is not a reason to add components that don't belong. Every component must still earn its place on its own merits.

The rule is: **the prose sections between components must never run to more than three in sequence without a visual break.**

If you can't find a legitimate component to insert at the 4th prose section, the issue is usually that prose sections need consolidating, not that a component is missing.

## The Reader's Internal Monologue — 9 questions

The typical internal monologue sequence for a cautious commercial reader follows this path:

1. **"Is this page actually relevant to my situation?"** — H1 and first intro paragraph close this.
2. **"Could my [property/business/use case] genuinely [outcome]?"** — Answer capsule and uplift component close this.
3. **"What's the catch?"** — Top CTA framing and worst-case figure close this.
4. **"What does [the business] actually do for [the fee]?"** — Services section closes this.
5. **"How do I know these figures are real?"** — Data panel, time-series chart, case study close this.
6. **"What if it doesn't work as well as they say?"** — Worst-case framing and structural reassurance close this.
7. **"Do I lose control of my [thing]?"** — Friction-reducer / control statement near CTA closes this.
8. **"Is running [the conversion] safe — will I be chased by a salesperson?"** — Disqualifier and low-friction CTA framing close this.
9. **"What do I actually do next?"** — Bottom CTA closes this.

### The rule

**If a section does not map to any of these nine questions, it is either in the wrong position or does not belong on the page.**

This is a hard test. Every section must be defensible against the monologue. A section that exists for branding, for word-count, for SEO without addressing one of the nine questions is a section that fails the engagement architecture.

### How the questions map to the three zones

| Zone | Questions addressed |
|---|---|
| Zone 1 — Possibility | Q1, Q2 |
| Zone 2 — Credibility | Q3, Q4, Q5 |
| Zone 3 — Commitment | Q6, Q7, Q8, Q9 |

The monologue progression and the zone progression are the same sequence viewed at two levels of resolution.

### Pre-publication mapping

Before declaring a page ready: list every section. Next to each, write the monologue question number it addresses. If any section has no question number, that section is on probation — either move it, rewrite it to address a question, or remove it.

## How the three rules interact

| Rule | What it ensures |
|---|---|
| Concept test | The page has a single sticky idea |
| Attention reset | The reader physically gets to the end |
| Reader monologue | Every section earns its place in the journey |

A page that passes all three:

- Has a proposition the reader can articulate (concept test)
- Has visual rhythm that supports a long scroll (attention reset)
- Has structure that maps to the reader's actual decision sequence (monologue)

A page that fails any one of the three:

- Reader can't articulate why they should care (concept test fail)
- Reader skims or leaves before the conversion (attention reset fail)
- Reader's specific question doesn't get answered (monologue fail)

## Common failures

### Failure 1 — Multiple competing concepts

The page argues both "more income" and "more flexibility" and "less hassle" — three propositions, no single sticky idea. Re-concept around one.

### Failure 2 — 5+ prose sections in sequence

Common in long-form guide pages. Insert visuals at the natural break points, or restructure to a Q&A format with visual answers.

### Failure 3 — Sections that don't map to any monologue question

A "Why Choose Us" section addresses none of the nine questions directly. Either rewrite it to address Q5 (proof) or Q6 (worst-case reassurance), or remove it.

### Failure 4 — Missing monologue questions

The page covers Q1, Q2, Q4, Q5, Q9 — but skips Q3 (the catch), Q6 (what if it doesn't work), Q7 (control), Q8 (sales friction). Conversion fails because the unaddressed questions are exactly the ones blocking action.

### Failure 5 — Wrong-zone monologue

Answering Q9 ("what do I do next?") in Zone 1, before Q1–Q8 have been addressed. The reader hasn't decided yet — the CTA is premature.

## How this applies to non-Stayful websites

### Concept test — adapts directly

For an e-commerce product: "This [product] is the only [category] that [unique benefit] — and ships in 24 hours."

For a SaaS tool: "Cut [task] from [old time] to [new time] for [audience], without [common downside]."

For a publisher: "The [topic] guide for [audience] — covering [specific scope] without [common bloat]."

### Attention reset — adapts directly

Product page: photos, spec tables, customer reviews, comparison tables, pricing modules.

SaaS page: screenshots, customer logos, ROI calculator, feature comparison, pricing tiers.

Publisher: pull quotes, charts, expert quote blocks, glossary callouts, related-article cards.

### Reader monologue — adapts with similar structure

Every commercial decision follows a similar nine-question monologue. The specific phrasing changes; the sequence is the same.

For a SaaS purchase:
1. Is this relevant to my role?
2. Could this solve my actual problem?
3. What's the catch?
4. What do I get for the price?
5. How do I know the claims are real?
6. What if it doesn't work?
7. Will it integrate with my stack?
8. Will I be chased by a salesperson if I sign up?
9. What's the next step?

The page must map. Sections that don't map to any question fail the architecture.
