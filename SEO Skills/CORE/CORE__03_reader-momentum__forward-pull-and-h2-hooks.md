# Forward Pull Principle and H2 Hook Rule

**Scope: Universal.** Every element on the page — every H2, every intro sentence, every callout label, every FAQ trigger — must do one of two things: **answer a question the reader already has, or create a question they now want answered.**

Elements that do neither are filler, regardless of word count or information value.

---

## The Forward Pull Principle

This is the test for every written element before it goes live:

> "Does this make the reader want to see what comes next — or does it give them a reason to stop?"

A section that fully satisfies the reader's question without pointing toward something they haven't yet seen gives them **permission to leave**. A section that answers their current question and raises the next one keeps them on the page.

Apply this test to:

- H1
- Every H2
- The intro paragraphs
- Callout box labels
- Pull stat labels
- FAQ trigger text
- The sentence immediately before every CTA

If any of these elements could be removed without the reader noticing they had lost momentum, the element needs rewriting.

---

## The H2 Micro-Hook Rule

**H2 headings must function as re-engagement hooks, not section labels.**

Every H2 must contain either:

1. A specific claim the reader wants verified, OR
2. An honest tension they want resolved, OR
3. A question implied by the section title that the section answers

If the H2 could be removed without the reader noticing they had lost anything, **it must be rewritten**.

## Label vs Hook — reference table

| Section | Label (never use) | Hook (always use) |
|---|---|---|
| Income comparison | "Income comparison" | "What this property type typically earns in [City] — including the quieter months" |
| Services | "What we manage" | "Everything Stayful handles — so you don't have to think about any of it" |
| How it works | "How it works" | "From enquiry to first booking — what the first 14 days look like" |
| Seasonality | "Seasonality in [City]" | "When [City] peaks, when it quiets, and what that means for your annual net figure" |
| Tax | "Tax changes" | "What the 2025 holiday let tax changes mean for your [City] property specifically" |
| Competitor comparison | "How we compare" | "What separates full-service management from a listing-only approach" |
| FAQ | "Frequently asked questions" | "The questions [City] landlords ask before they run the numbers" |
| Case study | "Owner example" | "What a comparable [City] property earned — in a strong month and a quiet one" |

These are reference examples, not fixed copy. Write fresh hooks for every page using the same principle.

## The H2 Writing Test

Apply this test before finalising every heading:

> Read the H2 in isolation. Ask: would a cautious reader who has just read the section above this heading want to read the section below it — because of this heading?

If the answer is uncertain, the heading is a label. Rewrite it as a hook.

### The three things every hook contains

A working H2 hook contains at least one of:

1. **A specific noun the reader cares about** (a city, a figure, a timeframe, a category)
2. **A tension the reader recognises** (the worry, the doubt, the alternative they're weighing)
3. **An implicit question they'd type into Google** (the search behind the search)

Hooks combining two of these usually outperform hooks with one. Hooks with all three are exceptional.

### Example breakdown

> "What a slower month looks like — and why it still beats a long-let"

- Specific noun: "a slower month" (the worry)
- Tension: "but does it still beat the alternative?"
- Implicit question: "what does a slow month look like for short-term letting?"

Three signals in one heading. The reader cannot skip this section without knowing whether the bad scenario still wins.

## What happens when H2s are labels

A page with label-style H2s:

- Loses scroll-depth (readers see "Services" and assume they know what's coming, then skim)
- Reduces engagement metrics (low time-per-section, low scroll-rate)
- Underperforms in search even with strong content (Google's engagement signals are part of ranking)
- Performs poorly in AI Overviews (the H2 doesn't signal what makes this section uniquely useful)

A page with hook-style H2s:

- Increases scroll depth (each new heading creates a new reason to keep going)
- Increases time on page (readers stop and read because the heading made them curious)
- Improves dwell time and reduces pogo-sticking (the page resolves what the heading raised)
- Performs better in AI Overviews (each H2 is a distinct claim worth citing)

## The forward pull test for elements other than H2s

### Intro paragraphs

Each paragraph should either answer a question the reader had or raise the next one they want answered. The 4-paragraph intro structure (`CORE__03_content__immediate-answer-and-intro-structure.md`) is designed to do this — direct answer, audience, honest framing, roadmap.

### Callout box labels

"KEY FIGURE" is generic. "WHAT LANDLORDS TYPICALLY NET" is specific. The latter creates a question the callout closes; the former describes what's in the box.

### Pull stat labels

The small label below a large number should describe what the number means in the reader's terms, not the business's. "Median net income across comparable Sheffield properties" is better than "Performance benchmark."

### FAQ trigger text

Already covered by the lead-language rule (`CORE__04_frameworks__objections-and-faq-language-framework.md`). The additional layer: the question must feel like something the reader is **already thinking**, not something the page introduces.

### The sentence before each CTA

The last sentence before the CTA closes the most likely remaining objection at that point in the reader's journey. After the income panel, the objection is the fee — so the CTA-preceding sentence addresses fees. After the FAQ, the objection is "am I ready" — so the CTA-preceding sentence addresses readiness ("Even if your property isn't available yet, running the numbers now means you go in with real figures.")

## Common failures

### Failure 1 — "Why Choose Stayful"

The single most common label-as-H2 violation. The reader doesn't want to read why to choose; they want to evaluate. Rewrite as: "What separates Stayful from a listing-only service — and what that means for your monthly net."

### Failure 2 — "About Us" / "Our Services"

Pages that follow the writer's table of contents instead of the reader's question sequence. Replace with reader-question H2s.

### Failure 3 — Generic transition sentences

The last sentence before each CTA being "Get started today" or "Run the numbers now." Replace with a sentence that closes the objection at that specific point in the page.

### Failure 4 — Curiosity-gap H2s that don't deliver

H2 promises specifics; section content is generic. The forward pull breaks. The reader feels misled and bounces.

### Failure 5 — All hook, no answer

Every H2 raises a question; none of them are closed. The page becomes a list of teasers. The reader eventually loses patience and leaves.

## How this applies to non-Stayful websites

The Forward Pull principle and H2 Hook rule are domain-neutral.

For an e-commerce product page:

| Label | Hook |
|---|---|
| "Specifications" | "What's different about this model — and why it matters for [use case]" |
| "Customer Reviews" | "What customers say after 6 months of use — including the criticisms" |
| "Shipping" | "When you'll receive it, what happens if it's the wrong fit" |

For a SaaS landing page:

| Label | Hook |
|---|---|
| "Features" | "Everything the platform handles — so you don't have to build it" |
| "Pricing" | "What you pay, what's included, and how it scales as you grow" |
| "Customers" | "Companies like yours — what they switched from and what they saw" |

In every industry, the rule is the same: would the reader want to read the next section because of this heading?
