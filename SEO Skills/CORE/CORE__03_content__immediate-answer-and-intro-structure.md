# Immediate Answer Principle and Intro Paragraph Structure

**Scope: Universal.** Every page must deliver information in a specific opening sequence. The opening intro paragraph must follow a four-part structure. Both rules are non-negotiable for service / commercial pages.

---

## The Immediate Answer Principle

Every page must deliver information in this exact sequence in the opening:

### 1. What this page is about — H1 and first intro paragraph

The reader must know within 3 seconds what they're looking at.

### 2. The direct answer — Answer capsule (50–70 words)

The primary question must be answered concisely, in the top half of the page, in a visually distinct block. The answer ends with a pointer to deeper detail elsewhere on the page — not a CTA, not a link.

### 3. The specific number — First data point

The first specific figure (income, occupancy, price, outcome) must appear before the top CTA block. Generic descriptions ("strong demand", "competitive rates") do not count — a specific number does.

### 4. The honest caveat — Worst-case in the same section as the headline

Whatever caveat or worst-case applies must appear in the same section as the headline figure, not buried lower on the page. This is what builds trust.

### 5. Everything else — All supporting detail

The middle and lower thirds of the page elaborate, but the reader has already received the core answer.

**This sequence is not flexible.** It is the single biggest engagement determinant: a page that buries the answer loses readers in the first 15 seconds.

---

## Intro paragraph structure

The opening intro section must **never be a single long paragraph**. It is structured in 3–4 short paragraphs.

### Paragraph 1 — Large font, the direct answer

One or two short punchy sentences that directly answer the reader's core question.

Example: "Short-term letting in Leeds typically nets 152–186% more than a comparable long-term tenancy. Stayful manages every part of it."

### Paragraph 2 — The specific situation

Two to three sentences naming the specific property/customer situations this page is written for.

Example: "This page is written for landlords currently on a long-term tenancy, landlords approaching a tenant's notice period, and owners of a Leeds property they're considering converting from long-let to short-let."

### Paragraph 3 — The honest framing

The question behind the question. What is the reader actually anxious about?

Example: "The real question isn't 'will it pay more?' — the answer to that is yes. The real question is 'will it pay more reliably?' This page gives you the honest answer to that, including what a slower month looks like."

### Paragraph 4 — Optional, sets up what's below

Sets the reader's expectation for what the rest of the page covers.

Example: "What follows is the full picture: typical and worst-case net income, what Stayful actually does to manage the property, the FHL tax changes that apply, and what running an income estimate will tell you."

### Length rule

**No intro paragraph should exceed 50 words.**

This forces clarity. A paragraph longer than 50 words has too many ideas to be the opening of a page where the reader is deciding whether to keep reading.

---

## Why this structure works

The reader scanning a service page has a specific question in mind. They want the answer fast, they want it specific, and they want to know what's not being said.

Conventional intros — "Welcome to our service" / "We are the leading provider of..." / a single paragraph mixing brand, service, and value prop — fail because they delay the answer. The reader leaves before getting to the part that would have closed the sale.

The 4-paragraph structure forces the writer to give the answer in P1, the audience in P2, the honesty in P3, and the roadmap in P4. The reader who survives P1 is hooked; the reader who reads P2 has self-identified as the target audience; the reader who reads P3 has accepted the honest framing and is now committed to reading the page.

---

## Common failures

### Failure 1 — The single-paragraph intro

One long opening paragraph that says everything at once. The reader scans it, doesn't find their specific situation named, and leaves. Even when all the right information is there.

### Failure 2 — Burying the figure

The headline data point doesn't appear until midway down the page. By then the reader has either pogo-sticked back to Google or accepted that this is another generic service page.

### Failure 3 — Worst-case in the FAQ only

The honest caveat lives only in the FAQ, after the reader has already decided whether to trust the page. Trust is established (or lost) in the opening. The worst-case has to appear there.

### Failure 4 — The intro that explains the business

"Stayful is a Leeds-based Airbnb management company offering professional services to property owners." This intro explains what Stayful is. It does not answer what the reader wants to know — what their property would earn.

### Failure 5 — Reverse-order intros

Opening with credibility (years in business, awards, team size) before the direct answer. Credibility matters but it does not earn the right to delay the answer.

## How this applies to non-Stayful websites

The principle and structure are domain-neutral.

For an e-commerce product page:
- P1: What this product does and the headline outcome
- P2: Who it's for (the specific use cases)
- P3: The honest tradeoff (what it isn't, who it isn't for)
- P4: What the rest of the page covers

For a SaaS landing page:
- P1: The outcome the software delivers, with a specific metric
- P2: The roles/companies this is built for
- P3: The honest framing of what the software is and isn't
- P4: Roadmap of what's below

For a publisher / guide:
- P1: The question the guide answers
- P2: Who the guide is for
- P3: Honest framing of what the guide covers in depth vs. lightly
- P4: Roadmap

The 50-word ceiling per paragraph and the answer-first sequence apply in every case.
