# Worst-Case Framing and Proof Points

**Scope: Universal copywriting principles. Specific Stayful instances in `STAYFUL__objections__worst-case-framing-language.md` and `STAYFUL__proof__levers-and-disqualifier.md`.**

Two related principles: how to present worst-case outcomes honestly (without losing the conversion), and how to choose and deploy proof points.

---

## Why worst-case framing matters

Every commercial page makes a claim. Every cautious reader assumes the page is showing the best case and discounts accordingly.

A page that proactively shows the worst case **before the reader has to ask** flips the trust equation. Instead of discounting the headline figure, the reader believes both numbers — because the page volunteered the worst-case one.

The mechanism: showing the worst case is a costly signal. Marketing copy hides downsides; honest analysis surfaces them. Readers recognise the difference instinctively, even if they don't articulate it.

---

## The five worst-case framing rules

### Rule 1 — Always show worst case alongside typical, never in isolation

**Correct format:**

> "In a slower year, this type of property in [city] typically nets £X per month. A comparable long-term tenancy would pay £Y. Even in the quieter period, short-term letting typically remains the stronger financial outcome."

This format has four components:
1. The worst-case figure (£X)
2. The comparison alternative (£Y)
3. The honest framing ("even in the quieter period")
4. The structural conclusion ("typically remains the stronger financial outcome")

All four must appear together. Splitting them weakens the proof.

### Rule 2 — Never fabricate a floor or imply a guarantee

The worst-case figure must be a real worst-case observed in actual data, not a marketing-derived "we never see worse than" claim.

If the data shows occasional outcomes below the worst-case quoted, the worst-case figure must be lowered or qualified.

A fabricated floor breaks the trust mechanism the entire framework depends on.

### Rule 3 — Follow worst-case with structural reassurance

After stating the worst case honestly, **immediately** explain why below-market performance is structurally unlikely.

For Stayful, the structural reassurance has two parts:

1. **Stayful consistently beats market average occupancy** (65–70% vs 55%)
2. **The 40% direct booking figure reduces platform dependency**

Combined into the exact phrasing:

> "Below-market performance would require two things to fail at once: the pricing and occupancy expertise we apply to every property, and the direct booking channel that currently accounts for 40% of our bookings."

This sentence does the work. It acknowledges that worst-case is possible (no guarantee implied) while structurally arguing it's unlikely.

### Rule 4 — Never lead with worst case

The order is always:

1. Typical figure
2. Worst-case figure
3. Structural reassurance
4. Comparison to the alternative

Leading with worst case creates a frame the reader can't escape. They never see the typical figure as the baseline; they see it as the optimistic claim.

Lead with the typical figure. Earn the right to show the worst case by establishing the baseline first.

### Rule 5 — The conversion form is the honest anchor

The exact language to use near the form:

> "The estimate shows you what this property type typically earns in your postcode, including what quieter months look like — not just the peak figure."

This re-anchors the entire page's promise: this is honest data, not marketing projections.

## The Income Guarantee Objection (Stayful-specific, framework-universal)

> "We don't guarantee a fixed income figure — and we'd be cautious of any company that does. What we show you is the realistic range, including quieter months, based on comparable properties in your postcode. Even in a slower year, the net figure typically exceeds what a long-term tenancy would pay."

This statement does five things:

1. **Denies the guarantee** (no income floor)
2. **Frames competitors who DO guarantee as untrustworthy** (a costly signal — Stayful would gain from making the same false claim)
3. **Refocuses on the realistic range** (what the page actually offers)
4. **Acknowledges the worst case** ("quieter months")
5. **Provides structural comfort** ("typically exceeds what a long-term tenancy would pay")

All five in three sentences. Apply the same five-part structure to any business's equivalent honesty statement.

## Proof Points

Proof points are the specific facts that make claims believable. Choose them carefully — fewer strong ones beat many weak ones.

### The six proof levers (Stayful)

1. **The income estimate itself** — the page's primary conversion
2. **Anonymised case studies** — always include a slower month figure
3. **The direct booking strategy** — frame as: "The estimate shows you year-one net income. The direct booking strategy is how that figure improves over time."
4. **Specific guest demand types** — hospital staff, contractors, university visitors, event attendees
5. **The 4.8 star Google rating**
6. **The 40% direct booking figure** — always explain the mechanism, not just the statistic

### How to choose proof points

For any business, proof points should be:

- **Specific** — a number, a named entity, a verifiable claim
- **Asymmetric** — something a competitor can't easily match
- **Mechanism-explained** — the proof works because the reader understands WHY

Generic proof points like "established in 2010" or "trusted by thousands" are weak because every competitor can claim equivalent statements. Specific proof like "40% of bookings come direct, not through Airbnb" is asymmetric because most competitors can't say this.

### The mechanism rule

Every statistic must be accompanied by the mechanism that produces it.

**Weak:** "We have 4.8 stars on Google."

**Strong:** "We have 4.8 stars on Google — most reviews come from owners after their first full quarter, when the income figures are real."

The mechanism gives the proof its power. A figure without context is a marketing claim. A figure with mechanism is evidence.

## The Disqualifier as Proof

A disqualifier is a statement that the service is NOT right for some readers. It functions as both honesty signal and conversion tool.

**For Stayful:** "If you need a guaranteed fixed amount each month regardless of bookings, short letting may not be the right fit."

This statement:

1. **Honestly disqualifies** a reader for whom short-let is wrong
2. **Implicitly qualifies** the readers for whom short-let is right
3. **Reduces bounce** from poorly-fit visitors
4. **Increases conversion** from well-fit visitors (they feel directly addressed)
5. **Builds trust** by demonstrating the business won't take just anyone

The disqualifier appears once on the page, near the primary CTA.

---

## Where each proof appears

| Proof | Position |
|---|---|
| Worst-case figure alongside typical | First mention of income; repeated near case study |
| Honest "no guarantee" statement | Body copy near CTA |
| Structural reassurance (occupancy + direct bookings) | Following every worst-case mention |
| 4.8 star rating | Naturally in body copy, not just a logo |
| 40% direct booking figure | Body copy + FAQ with mechanism |
| Anonymised case study | Mid-page (Zone 2) — credibility section |
| Disqualifier | Near primary CTA only |

## Common failures

### Failure 1 — Worst case in isolation

Showing the slow-month figure as a standalone callout without the typical figure or comparison. Reader anchors on the bad number.

### Failure 2 — Fabricated worst case

A worst-case figure that doesn't match observed data. Eventually a reader hits a worse outcome and trust collapses across all pages.

### Failure 3 — Worst case followed by sales push

The worst-case figure followed immediately by "But run your estimate now!" The transition feels like deflection. Insert the structural reassurance before any CTA.

### Failure 4 — Multiple proof points stacked without mechanism

"4.8 stars, 70+ properties, £3M+ revenue, 40% direct, 65-70% occupancy" — a wall of statistics. Each has more weight when explained individually with its mechanism.

### Failure 5 — Missing the disqualifier

Trying to convert every reader regardless of fit. Increases bounce, reduces post-conversion satisfaction, hurts long-term reputation.

## How this applies to non-Stayful websites

The framework is domain-neutral.

**E-commerce:**
- Worst case: "Here's what happens if it doesn't fit / arrives damaged / doesn't work as expected" with the return policy as structural reassurance
- Proof: customer count, repeat purchase rate, average rating with verified reviewer count
- Disqualifier: "If you need [specific feature this product doesn't have], this isn't the right model for you"

**SaaS:**
- Worst case: "Here's what happens if your team doesn't adopt it" with onboarding support as structural reassurance
- Proof: retention rate, NPS, ROI data from customer case studies with named companies
- Disqualifier: "If you have fewer than [N] team members, the free plan covers you and you don't need this tier"

**Service business:**
- Worst case: "Here's what happens if the project takes longer than estimated" with the SLA or fixed-fee structure as reassurance
- Proof: project completion rate, client retention, named case studies with results
- Disqualifier: "If your timeline is under [N] weeks, we're probably not the right fit for the scope you need"

The five worst-case rules and the proof-with-mechanism rule apply equally.
